Yes, training taekwondo burns energy, and quite a lot: it is a vigorous-intensity activity. But the exact number depends on who is training and what each class involves. Here is a simple and honest estimate.

{{< figura src="img/ilustracoes/pontape.svg" alt="Drawing of a taekwondo practitioner performing a high kick, with a flame beside them." >}}

## A calculation you can do at home

Researchers measure the effort of an activity in **METs**, a unit that compares the energy spent with the energy spent at rest. The reference table, the *2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities*, has no single value for taekwondo: it has values for martial arts depending on the pace. For a slow pace with beginner practitioners (code 15425) it gives **5.3 METs**. For a moderate pace, which expressly includes taekwondo (code 15430), it gives **10.3 METs**. There is also a dedicated entry for taekwondo combat simulation (code 15432), at **14.3 METs**.

To estimate the calories of one hour of continuous effort, multiply:

**METs × weight (kg) × duration (hours)**

{{< figura src="img/ilustracoes/formula.svg" alt="Diagram: METs times weight in kilograms times hours equals kilocalories. Example: 10.3 times 70 times 1 gives about 721 kilocalories." largura="800" altura="300" >}}

For a 70 kg adult, that gives about **371 kcal** per hour at a slow pace, about **721 kcal** per hour at a moderate pace and about **1001 kcal** per hour in combat simulation. That last figure is for continuous, very intense effort, which does not match a normal class.

## Why the real result is usually different

- **A class is not an hour of continuous effort.** There are warm-ups, explanations, breaks and stretching, so the average energy spent in a class tends to be lower than these calculations.
- **Weight and fitness matter.** Heavier people spend more; people who have trained for longer are usually more efficient.
- **The type of training changes the effort.** **Kyorugi** (sparring) and drills with many kick repetitions are more demanding than **poomsae** (sequences of movements, or "forms") performed slowly. Scientific studies have measured the effort of both, and taekwondo is described as an intermittent sport, with peaks of intensity and short breaks.
- **Children are different.** The values above are for adults. For younger people, the aim of training is coordination, discipline and enjoying the practice, not counting calories.

## What matters more than calories

Training regularly, two or three times a week, counts for more than the energy spent in a single class. Besides physical effort, taekwondo works on flexibility, balance, coordination and concentration. Anyone with a health condition, or who has not exercised for a long time, should talk to a doctor first.

No training, on its own, guarantees weight loss: food and rest are part of the result.
